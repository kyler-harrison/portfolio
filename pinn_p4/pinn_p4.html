PINNs Part 4: Solving the Helium Atom Schrodinger Equation

01/22/2026

NOTE very very incredibly rough draft

In Part 3, the Hydrogen atom Schrodinger equation was approximately solved with a PINN. In this writeup I aim to solve the Helium atom Schrodinger equation with a PINN. Helium is the next element on the periodic table, with an atomic number of 2, meaning a neutral Helium atom has 2 protons and 2 electrons. The addition of a second electron makes the Schrodinger equation of the Helium atom unsolvable analytically (at least, nobody has come up with an analytical solution yet).


The Helium atom Schrodinger equation

The He atom has two electrons, but only one can be in an excited state at a time, while the other electron remains in the ground state.

The Hamiltonian presented previously for Hydrogen can be generalized to atoms with $Z$ electrons

$$\begin{equation} \hat{H} = \sum_{j=1}^Z \left\{ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla_j^2 - \left( \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0}\right) \frac{Ze^2}{|\vec{r}_j|} \right\} + \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \right) \sum_{j \neq k}^Z \frac{e^2}{|\vec{r}_j - \vec{r}_k|} . \end{equation}$$

For Helium in specific, the Hamiltonian is

$$\begin{equation} \hat{H} = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla_1^2 - \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2e^2}{|\vec{r}_1|} \right) + \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla_2^2 - \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2e^2}{|\vec{r}_2|} \right) + \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{e^2}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|} . \end{equation}$$

The term that involves the interaction of the two electrons, $\frac{e^2}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|}$, makes the problem much harder to solve. When this Hamiltonian is applied to the Schrodinger equation, we get

$$\begin{equation} -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \left(\nabla_1^2 \psi + \nabla_2^2 \psi \right) - \frac{e^2}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \left(\frac{2}{|\vec{r}_1|} + \frac{2}{|\vec{r}_2|} - \frac{1}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|} \right) \psi = E \psi. \end{equation}$$

For ease of implementation in code, I'll expand the gradients

$$\begin{equation} -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \left(\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x_1^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial y_1^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial z_1^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x_2^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial y_2^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial z_2^2} \right) - \frac{e^2}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \left(\frac{2}{|\vec{r}_1|} + \frac{2}{|\vec{r}_2|} - \frac{1}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|} \right) \psi = E \psi. \end{equation}$$

Now, the PINN will take the positions of two electrons as input instead of the single $x$, $y$, $z$ electron position in the H problem.


Approximation methods

Since the He Schrodinger can't be solved analytically, an approximation method must be used to estimate its solution. There are several common approaches that yield different levels of accuracy in their estimates for the energy level and wavefunction of He.

Perturbation theory and the variational method are often used to calculate estimates of the energy levels of He (see Griffiths chapters 5 and 8). Perturbation theory provides the wavefunction and energy for any energy level, while the variational method only provides the energy of the ground state. I decided to focus on perturbation theory since it looked like it would provide a more useful output (any wavefunction and corresponding energy level). There are other applicable numerical approximation techniques, but I didn't spend much time exploring other options as perturbation theory looked good enough.

Perturbation theory works by first assuming that a trial wavefunction solves the He Schrodinger. The trial wavefunction is simply two H atom wavefunctions multiplied by each other. This wavefunction ignores interactions between the two electrons in He, and gives an inaccurate result. Perturbation theory perturbs that wavefunction with slight corrections in order to get closer to the correct wavefunction and energy value. The correction can be calculated to an arbitrarily high-order, but Griffiths suggests that a 2nd order correction is all that's really ever necessary in practice. I tried and failed to find any resources that implemented perturbation theory to the first few energy levels of He to the 2nd order of correction, and resolved to figure out how to do it on my own.

To put it lightly, the perturbation theory approximation technique is difficult to understand and implement. I spent a couple dozen hours working through perturbation theory in order to come up with a practical way to use it in the context of this problem, and I think I developed an understanding of how to do so, but I'm still not very confident in some of my assumptions. Ultimately, I decided it wasn't worth the effort to spend more time trying to generate approximations for the first few energy levels of Helium as the work was starting to exceed the scope of this project.

Without "correct" wavefunctions and energy values to compare the PINN's output to, it's hard to say how "good" the PINN's predictions are. Furthermore, my understanding of quantum theory beyond the Hydrogen atom is quite limited. I honestly don't understand much about multi-electron atoms because that's just chemistry and nobody likes chemistry. Further furthermore, I decided that I wanted to finally conclude this project, so, I settled on just training a PINN to learn the ground state of the Helium atom. The ground state energy of He has been reliably measured to be around $E = -79 \text{ eV}$. Similar to H, the ground state wavefunction of He is expected to show decay as the radius of each electron increases. Along with the loss calculation of how well the PINN's predictions fit the Schrodinger equation, I think I have enough info to pass judgement on the results.


My PINN approach

Similar to the Hydrogen PINN, I generated a sphere of points of a fixed radius to use as input data. Helium has two electrons, which adds three more dimensions to the input space. If I used the same data generation scheme as for H, the amount of input data would be squared. Since the H data had around $10^4$ input points, this means the He data would have $10^8$ input points, an impractical amount. To combat this, I simply sampled what I reasoned to be a significant amount of points for each electron from the full sphere boundary condition and collocation sets, and then concatenated the positions together to create an input with size on the order of $10^4$. This might present a potential limitation in the network's ability to learn, but I'm pretty confident this is enough data.

On the assumption that the PINN approach used to solve the H Schrodinger equation is valid, I used the same loss terms previously defined.

From the Schrodinger equation, we can write

$$\begin{equation} f = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \left(\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x_1^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial y_1^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial z_1^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x_2^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial y_2^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial z_2^2} \right) - \frac{e^2}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \left(\frac{2}{|\vec{r}_1|} + \frac{2}{|\vec{r}_2|} - \frac{1}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|} \right) \psi - E \psi, \end{equation}$$

Again, the overall loss is defined

$$\begin{equation} L = L_{f} + L_{bc} + L_{trivial} . \end{equation}$$


PINN results

To keep things simple, I decided to train PINNs on values around the known $E = -79 \text{ eV}$ energy level. In theory, I could have scanned over a much wider range of $E$ values and hoped to see the best convergence around the correct energy, but at this point I was just trying to test the limitations of the approach I've used for solving simpler partial differential equations described previously.

I somewhat arbitrarily set the PINN to train on $E$ values of $-74, -79, -84 \text{ eV}$. In Coulomb units, these values convert to approximately $-2.72, -2.9, -3.09$, respectively. I expected the best PINN convergence to occur with $E = -2.9$.

Each PINN ($E$ value) was trained for 10,000 steps, which took around 27 minutes (~80 total minutes) on my computer.

The loss values suggest that this PINN did not converge to a solution nearly as well as in previously discussed problems. Each $E$ reached an overall $L$ value on the order of $10^1$ at best, and the PINN did not minimize $L$ with the correct $E = -2.9$. That being said, the predicted wavefunctions do roughly meet the expectation of decay as the electrons are placed farther from the nucleus (origin).


Limitations of my PINN approach

As I've previously discussed, the PINN approach I've used here appears to be limited by the size of the input data, the complexity of the differential equation to be solved, and the amount of available compute resources and time. Fundamentally, I still don't like the scan method I've used to determine correct $E$ eigenvalues. The technique feels simplistic and is definitely a big inefficiency considering that the entire neural network must be trained for each value of $E$ in your search space. The Helium PINN problem presented herein greatly shows the limitations of my method. The amount of possible input data is large, the training time for each PINN begins to approach an unreasonable duration, and the $E$ search space is much larger since we don't have actual theoretical values to use as a basis for predictions.

Although simplistic, the $E$ scan method did prove effective for solving simpler differential equations as shown in Part 2 and Part 3. I believe the PINN's failure to achieve good convergence can be mainly contributed to a lack of enough data, train time, and the complexity of He's Schrodinger equation. Along with these shortcomings, better loss terms and regularization penalties could probably be defined. My limited and somewhat naive understanding of the quantum reality of multi-electron atoms has also definitely contributed to some inaccuracies and deficiencies that I'm not even completely aware of.

Despite the shortcomings of this PINN's results, I'm still happy to see that it did learn something. Qualitatively, the predicted wavefunctions align with theoretical expectations, even if the resemblance is crude.
